Lately, I've been thinking about a particular conundrum: "What if everyone's age were obviously displayed on the internet? How would that change the internet's opinion landscape? And how much better or worse would it make the internet?"

Before we continue, it is important to mention that there is no non-intrusive way to make everyone's age visible over the keyboard. Hence, this blog is not about whether we should have age verification or not. The topic today is how it would hypothetically be if a person's opinion over the internet were weighed based on their real-life age.

Now, let's imagine a scenario where a child, around 10 to 11 years old, comes up to you and says something controversial. Would you take those words seriously? Conversely, due to the anonymous nature of the web, every sentence and every opinion are weighed the same and treated the same way, regardless of who says them. And children, they are not always guaranteed to act nicely or be well taught. They could be saying things for the sake of saying them to get engagement.

If, through public blogs and town squares such as Twitter and Facebook, everyone's age were visible, those controversial statements would be ignored and put aside, similar to what an adult would do in real life, or the kid would be scolded and told not to say that again. In my opinion, the internet would be a better place than what it is now, thanks to the lowered barrier to thinking critically when it comes to reading people's statements online, as well as the fact that it would be much easier to keep children out of adult spaces where a child has no right to be.

Now, let's address the elephant in the room. As mentioned before, I believe that everyone knowing each other's age would make the internet a better place. On the other hand, achieving such an accomplishment without changing the nature of cyberspace as a whole is impossible. I strongly believe that the change must not be on the internet itself but on a societal level. Parents and guardians of children have an especially important role in controlling what their children see and do on the internet, similar to how you wouldn't let a stranger take your children by the hand and lead them anywhere.

At the end of the day, the internet was initially built for adults and critical thinkers to use. When we, as a collective, opened the internet up to the majority, we set ourselves up to solve the problem of not letting children into adult spaces and moderating ourselves both in real life and over the internet.
